I
Section I

Preamble and Scope

This brief examines and systematically refutes every legal defence advanced, or plausibly advanceable, by Senator Ronald Marapon Dela Rosa in opposition to the arrest warrant issued against him by Pre-Trial Chamber I of the International Criminal Court on 6 November 2025 and unsealed on 11 May 2026.

The warrant charges Dela Rosa as an indirect co-perpetrator under Article 25(3)(a) of the Rome Statute for the crime against humanity of murder, committed at least between 3 July 2016 and the end of April 2018, resulting in no fewer than 32 identified killings in the Republic of the Philippines during his tenure as Chief of the Philippine National Police.

The defences addressed herein arise from: (a) Dela Rosa's formal petition before the Philippine Supreme Court, docketed as GR No. 278747, filed March 2025 and supplemented on 11 May 2026; (b) Senate Resolution No. 44, the "Filipino Protection from Extraordinary Rendition Resolution," adopted 17 March 2026; (c) public statements by Dela Rosa's lead counsel, Atty. Israelito Torreon; and (d) arguments derivable from first principles under Philippine constitutional law, international criminal law, and treaty law.

Each defence is stated in its strongest possible form before being refuted. No strawman construction has been employed. Citations are to primary instruments, binding judicial decisions, and authoritative legal commentary.

Section III

The Ten Defences — Systematic Refutation

Defence 01 The ICC has no jurisdiction because the Philippines withdrew from the Rome Statute in 2019
The Argument

The Philippines deposited its withdrawal notification on 17 March 2018. Withdrawal took effect one year later under Article 127(1). The Philippines is no longer a state party and bears no cooperation obligation. Any warrant against a Philippine national is an exercise of jurisdiction the court no longer holds.

Refutation

This argument has been litigated to final appellate determination and rejected. Article 127(2) of the Rome Statute states explicitly:

"A State shall not be discharged, by reason of its withdrawal, from the obligations arising from this Statute while it was a Party to the Statute... nor shall it prejudice in any way the continued consideration of any matter which was already under consideration by the Court prior to the date on which the withdrawal became effective."

The ICC Appeals Chamber confirmed on 22 April 2026 — nineteen days before the warrant was unsealed — that the court retains jurisdiction over all crimes committed on Philippine territory between 1 November 2011 and 16 March 2019, encompassing the entirety of the charged period. The chamber unanimously rejected this argument in The Prosecutor v. Duterte. The Philippine Supreme Court's own ruling in Pangilinan v. Cayetano (2021) separately holds that withdrawal "does not discharge a state party from the obligations it has incurred as a member." This argument is res judicata at both international and domestic appellate level.

Holding FAILS. Jurisdiction over the membership period is confirmed at Appeals Chamber level. The argument has been litigated to finality and cannot be relitigated without contradicting binding precedent.
Defence 02 The Philippine Supreme Court must issue prior domestic judicial authorisation before any arrest can occur
The Argument

Counsel Torreon has argued before the Supreme Court that "any contemplated arrest, detention, custody, transfer, turnover, rendition, or surrender... shall, at the very least, be subject to prior Philippine judicial authorization and to the procedural safeguards embodied in the 2025 Rules on Extradition Proceedings." No domestic court has made a probable cause determination. Therefore no arrest is lawful.

Refutation

This argument rests on a category error: the conflation of surrender to an international tribunal with extradition to a foreign state. They are legally distinct proceedings.

Extradition involves one sovereign state requesting transfer from another, typically through bilateral treaty. Extradition proceedings properly trigger domestic judicial review. Surrender to the ICC is categorically different — the ICC is an international judicial body whose jurisdiction the Philippines voluntarily accepted by ratifying the Rome Statute. The Pre-Trial Chamber has already conducted a judicial assessment and found reasonable grounds. That finding is the judicial determination of probable cause. Requiring Philippine courts to re-run it is procedural duplication designed to create delay, not due process.

Republic Act 9851, Section 17 provides:

"The Philippines shall, to the extent possible, cooperate with requests for arrest and surrender made by a competent international court or tribunal in cases involving any of the crimes defined in this Act... The Philippines may surrender or extradite, as appropriate, any person charged or convicted of any crime defined herein."

The DOJ confirmed in May 2026 that RA 9851 constitutes sufficient domestic authority for surrender without a prior court order — the identical basis used for Duterte's transfer in March 2025. The 2025 Rules on Extradition cited by counsel govern extradition, not ICC surrender. They are inapplicable.

Holding FAILS. ICC surrender and extradition are legally distinct. The Pre-Trial Chamber's probable cause finding is the operative judicial determination. RA 9851 provides domestic authority without a prior court order. The 2025 Extradition Rules do not apply.
Defence 03 The 1987 Constitution's due process clause bars surrender without domestic judicial clearance
The Argument

Article III, Section 1 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution provides that no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law. Surrendering Dela Rosa to the ICC without a Philippine court order is an unchecked executive act. This violates the constitutional separation of powers and the right to liberty.

Refutation

Constitutional due process is not violated when the government executes a surrender obligation arising from a treaty validly ratified under Article VII, Section 21 of the same Constitution. The Senate ratified the Rome Statute; the treaty is part of Philippine law. RA 9851 was enacted by Congress in implementation of that treaty. Executive enforcement of a legislative mandate implementing a constitutional treaty obligation is not a due process violation. It is the constitutional order functioning as designed.

The argument also proves too much. If it were correct, the Philippines could never cooperate with any international criminal tribunal, rendering RA 9851 a constitutional nullity — which the legislature that enacted it cannot have intended.

The argument contains an internal contradiction. Dela Rosa was a petitioner in the challenge to Duterte's March 2025 arrest, GR No. 278747. The Supreme Court declined to issue a TRO then; Duterte was transferred without a prior court order; the case remains pending without resolution adverse to the government. If constitutional due process categorically barred ICC surrender, the Supreme Court would have acted with urgency in March 2025. Its inaction is precedentially adverse to Dela Rosa's current position.

Holding FAILS. Treaty obligations validly ratified are part of the constitutional order. RA 9851 is a valid legislative implementation. No prior court order is constitutionally required. The March 2025 Duterte transfer — unchallenged by TRO — is adverse precedent.
Defence 04 The complementarity principle requires the ICC to defer to Philippine domestic jurisdiction
The Argument

Article 17 of the Rome Statute provides that the ICC may exercise jurisdiction only when the state "is unwilling or unable genuinely to carry out the investigation or prosecution." Philippine courts exist and function. The state is neither unwilling nor unable. The ICC is therefore precluded from acting.

Refutation

The complementarity inquiry is not answered by showing that Philippine courts formally exist. Article 17(2) defines "unwilling" as encompassing proceedings "undertaken for the purpose of shielding the person concerned from criminal responsibility" or "conducted in a manner inconsistent with an intent to bring the person concerned to justice."

The factual record is dispositive. The Philippine government under Duterte actively shielded the drug war's architects. The PNP systematically destroyed operational records. Witnesses were killed, intimidated, or recanted. The DOJ under Marcos — having opened its own review — explicitly concluded that domestic accountability was impossible due to destroyed or absent evidence and formally ceded prosecution to the ICC. The government's own concession of domestic incapacity is the predicate for the complementarity finding in the prosecution's favour.

The Philippines filed a deferral request under Article 18(2) in November 2021 — acknowledging ICC jurisdiction — which was rejected by the court in June 2022 after finding domestic proceedings inadequate. The complementarity challenge has been litigated to finality at ICC appellate level in the Duterte proceedings and rejected.

Holding FAILS. Complementarity is not satisfied by institutional existence. The Philippine government's own admission of domestic incapacity is dispositive. The Article 18(2) deferral rejection forecloses this argument.
Defence 05 Dela Rosa enjoys legislative immunity under Article VI, Section 11 of the 1987 Constitution
The Argument

Article VI, Section 11 of the 1987 Constitution provides that senators shall be privileged from arrest "in all offenses punishable by not more than six years imprisonment" during attendance at sessions and while going to and from the legislature. Dela Rosa is a sitting senator present at a Senate session on 11 May 2026.

Refutation

The argument fails on the face of the constitutional text it invokes. The immunity clause is expressly limited to offences punishable by not more than six years. The ICC warrant charges Dela Rosa with crimes against humanity. Under RA 9851, Sections 5 and 7, crimes against humanity carry a penalty of reclusion perpetua — 20 to 40 years imprisonment. The constitutional immunity is facially inapplicable.

Senate Resolution No. 44 cannot expand a constitutional privilege beyond its textual scope. A legislative body may not by resolution override the constitutional text that defines and limits its own members' immunity. This is not a contested proposition of constitutional law.

Furthermore, legislative immunity under Article VI, Section 11 was designed to protect legislators from politically motivated arrest by the executive during legislative activity. It was never construed, in any Philippine constitutional decision, as conferring immunity from valid international criminal process for crimes committed while exercising command authority over the national police.

Holding FAILS. The immunity clause is expressly limited to offences carrying six years or less. Crimes against humanity carry reclusion perpetua. Senate Resolution No. 44 cannot expand a constitutional privilege beyond its stated textual scope.
Defence 06 The Senate may place Dela Rosa under "protective custody" as an exercise of institutional prerogative
The Argument

Senate President Cayetano declared Dela Rosa under "protective custody" following the NBI's attempted service on 11 May 2026. The Senate, as a co-equal branch, has inherent authority to protect its members from executive overreach and to preserve the integrity of its legislative functions.

Refutation

Five senators repudiated this in a formal resolution stating the chamber "possesses no constitutional or statutory authority to provide protective custody or sanctuary from lawful arrest, surrender, or judicial processes." This dissenting view is legally correct on every applicable instrument.

No provision of the 1987 Constitution grants the Senate authority to shelter members from criminal arrest. The immunity provisions of Article VI, Section 11 are specific and limited — they create no general power of sanctuary. Parliamentary privilege in no Westminster jurisdiction has ever been construed to permit the legislature to obstruct valid criminal process against its members.

Senate Resolution No. 44's characterisation of the ICC process as "extraordinary rendition" is legally incoherent. Extraordinary rendition is the extrajudicial, covert transfer of a person without legal process — the antithesis of what an ICC warrant represents. A formal judicial proceeding by a three-judge international chamber is not extraordinary rendition by any definition in international law.

The separation of powers argument cuts against the Senate. Obstructing the executive's cooperation with a valid international judicial warrant — while the executive is implementing a legislative mandate under RA 9851 — is itself constitutionally suspect. The Senate is attempting to prevent the executive from obeying the law the Senate enacted.

Holding FAILS. No constitutional or statutory authority for Senate sanctuary exists. Senate Resolution No. 44 mischaracterises ICC process. The Senate's obstruction of an executive act mandated by legislation the Senate passed is itself constitutionally questionable.
Defence 07 The warrant violates due process because Dela Rosa has had no opportunity to contest the charges
The Argument

Due process requires notice and an opportunity to be heard before any deprivation of liberty. Dela Rosa has not appeared before the ICC, has not been represented in warrant proceedings, and has had no opportunity to challenge the evidentiary basis for his arrest. The warrant was issued ex parte. This violates fundamental fair trial guarantees.

Refutation

This argument conflates arrest with conviction and mistakes the procedural stage of proceedings. Arrest warrants are, by their nature, issued ex parte on the basis of probable cause. This is universal across legal systems, including the Philippines. Philippine Rules of Criminal Procedure provide for warrants to issue upon judicial finding of probable cause after ex parte assessment — no prior notice to the accused is required or appropriate, because prior notice would defeat the warrant's enforcement purpose.

The ICC warrant was issued pursuant to Article 58 of the Rome Statute, which requires the Pre-Trial Chamber to find "reasonable grounds to believe" the person committed the crimes — the international equivalent of probable cause. Three judges assessed the prosecution's material and made that finding. Due process at the arrest stage is satisfied.

Dela Rosa's rights to contest charges, challenge evidence, cross-examine witnesses, and present a defence arise after surrender — at the confirmation of charges hearing under Part V and at trial under Part VI of the Rome Statute. These rights are fully guaranteed. They follow from arrest. The argument, taken to its conclusion, would make all arrest warrants unconstitutional in all jurisdictions. No legal system accepts this.

Holding FAILS. Arrest warrants are inherently ex parte. The Pre-Trial Chamber's finding satisfies probable cause. Adversarial rights arise at subsequent proceedings and are fully guaranteed under the Rome Statute.
Defence 08 Sovereignty: the Philippines cannot be compelled to surrender its own citizen to a foreign body
The Argument

The Philippines is a sovereign state. The surrender of a Filipino citizen to an international court without a Philippine court order is an affront to national sovereignty. No foreign body can compel the Philippines to hand over its own nationals.

Refutation

Sovereignty is not a licence to breach voluntarily assumed treaty obligations. The Philippines exercised its sovereignty when it ratified the Rome Statute in 2011. That exercise of sovereign will created binding obligations. A state cannot simultaneously invoke sovereignty to justify joining a treaty and invoke sovereignty to justify departing from the obligations that treaty creates when they become inconvenient for specific individuals.

The ICC is not a "foreign body" in the relevant sense. It is an international judicial institution created by a multilateral treaty to which the Philippines was party. Its jurisdiction was accepted by the Philippine Senate, ratified by the President, and upheld by the Supreme Court. Moreover, RA 9851 — a Philippine Act, enacted by Filipino legislators, signed by a Filipino president — provides the domestic legal basis for surrender. The Philippines is implementing its own law, not submitting to foreign compulsion.

The March 2025 Duterte transfer is dispositive: the Philippines surrendered its former president to the ICC. No sovereignty crisis resulted. The Philippines remains fully sovereign. Compliance with a treaty obligation voluntarily undertaken has never, in any jurisdiction, been held to constitute a sovereignty violation.

Holding FAILS. Sovereignty is expressed through treaty ratification, not through breach. RA 9851 is Philippine law. Compliance with Philippine law is not a sovereignty violation. The Duterte transfer provides operative precedent.
Defence 09 The killings were lawful police operations — no crime against humanity occurred
The Argument

The drug war operations were lawful enforcement actions against armed suspects who resisted arrest. Deaths in the course of law enforcement are not crimes against humanity. The ICC is criminalising legitimate state security policy.

Refutation

The Pre-Trial Chamber assessed this claim in examining the warrant application and found it unsupported. The chamber's probable cause finding explicitly encompasses the determination that the killings constituted a "widespread and systematic attack against civilians pursuant to a state policy targeting alleged drug offenders" — the contextual elements of crimes against humanity under Article 7 of the Rome Statute. A widespread, systematic attack of this character cannot be characterised as routine law enforcement.

Available forensic evidence corroborates this finding: post-mortem analysis in documented cases revealed bound wrists, execution-style gunshot wounds, and absence of weapon residue on victims — inconsistent with police operations involving armed resistance, consistent with extrajudicial execution. The Philippine Senate's own hearings produced testimony establishing systematic kill operations. Nine domestic criminal convictions in a decade, from an estimated death toll in the tens of thousands, is itself evidentiary of systematic impunity rather than lawful enforcement.

The argument also misapprehends the mode of liability. Dela Rosa is charged as an indirect co-perpetrator under Article 25(3)(a) — for his alleged role in a common plan executed through and by means of others. The "lawful operations" characterisation speaks to the conduct of field officers. It does not address the command structure and common plan for which Dela Rosa bears alleged responsibility at the level charged.

Holding FAILS. The Pre-Trial Chamber made a probable cause finding covering the contextual elements of crimes against humanity. The forensic and testimonial record in the public domain corroborates it. Dela Rosa's liability is alleged at the command-structure level, not for individual field executions.
Defence 10 The warrant is politically motivated — driven by Trillanes, the Marcos administration, and anti-Duterte forces
The Argument

Antonio Trillanes, a political opponent of Duterte and Dela Rosa, filed the original ICC complaint. The Marcos administration surrendered Duterte for political reasons. The warrant against Dela Rosa is a continuation of factional political warfare. The court has been weaponised by domestic political actors.

Refutation

The political motivations of complainants do not determine the legal validity of prosecutorial findings or judicial determinations. The ICC Office of the Prosecutor and Pre-Trial Chamber are independent of the parties who filed complaints. The prosecution conducted its own investigation, gathered its own evidence, and applied for a warrant based on independent assessment. The Pre-Trial Chamber independently assessed that material. Neither is accountable to Trillanes, Marcos, or any other political actor.

The ICC's conduct across this proceeding demonstrates institutional independence from political pressure. It rejected the Philippines' Article 18(2) deferral request despite diplomatic pressure. It maintained jurisdiction despite withdrawal. It issued a warrant in November 2025 — months before the current political crisis — while keeping it sealed, inconsistent with an institution responding to momentary political convenience.

The argument has a decisive temporal problem: if the warrant reflects a Marcos-Trillanes political coordination, one must account for why it was issued and sealed in November 2025, months before any of the current events, and why the charge architecture traces to evidence gathered before Marcos took office in 2022.

Finally: even if every political actor who contributed to the ICC's attention had done so for purely self-interested reasons, this is legally irrelevant to whether the crimes occurred. Motives of complainants cannot exculpate accused parties where independent evidence of criminal conduct is sufficient.

Holding FAILS. Complainants' motives are irrelevant to independent judicial findings. The ICC has demonstrated institutional independence in this proceeding. The warrant predates current political events by months. Evidence of crimes is independent of those who surfaced it.

Section IV

Cumulative Assessment

No defence advanced by Dela Rosa, or advanceable from first principles, survives analytical scrutiny. The pattern of failure is consistent across categories:

  • 01ICC jurisdiction (withdrawal) FAILS — litigated to appellate finality 22 April 2026
  • 02Prior domestic court order FAILS — ICC surrender ≠ extradition; RA 9851 is sufficient
  • 03Constitutional due process FAILS — treaty ratification is part of constitutional order; adverse SC precedent
  • 04Complementarity FAILS — DOJ's own admission of incapacity forecloses it; deferral request rejected 2022
  • 05Legislative immunity FAILS — expressly limited to <6-year offences; crimes against humanity = reclusion perpetua
  • 06Senate protective custody FAILS — no constitutional or statutory basis; repudiated by 5 senators
  • 07Ex parte / fair trial FAILS — arrest warrants are universally ex parte; adversarial rights arise post-surrender
  • 08Sovereignty FAILS — treaty ratification is the exercise of sovereignty; Duterte precedent is operative
  • 09Lawful operations FAILS — Pre-Trial Chamber found probable cause on crimes against humanity elements; wrong mode of liability
  • 10Political motivation FAILS — legally irrelevant; warrant predates political events; institutional independence demonstrated

What remains when all defences are stripped away: a valid warrant by a three-judge chamber after independent probable cause assessment; a domestic statute providing the enforcement mechanism; a binding appellate ruling confirming jurisdiction; an adverse Supreme Court precedent; and an operative domestic transfer precedent from March 2025. No court — domestic or international — that has considered these arguments has accepted them.

The men demanding due process are the men who denied it to thousands of people killed on neighbourhood watchlists without charge, without trial, without legal remedy of any kind. The arguments are not without formal structure. Their moral architecture is hollow.

V
Section V

Structural Observation: The Purpose of Losing Arguments

Analytical Note

This section departs from strict legal analysis to address the strategic function of the defences examined above. It is included because understanding that function is essential to assessing the situation correctly.

The ten defences were constructed by experienced Philippine constitutional lawyers and are, in the main, carefully assembled. They are not frivolous. They are wrong — but wrong in ways that require careful engagement, which is precisely their design purpose.

Each defence, even when defeated on the merits, produces delay. Each Supreme Court petition, even when denied, generates procedural suspension. Each TRO application, each supplemental motion, each 72-hour comment period — all consume institutional bandwidth and keep the situation in temporal suspension. In the three days between the warrant's unsealing and Dela Rosa's pre-dawn departure from the Senate compound, the defences had achieved their purpose: he was not arrested.

The defences function not primarily as arguments designed to prevail but as instruments of temporal warfare against an enforcement mechanism that depends entirely on state cooperation. The ICC has no police force. It cannot break down a legislature door. It can issue warrants and wait. Every day the Philippine state declines to act is a day the defences have succeeded, regardless of their legal merit.

The legal battle is partly a proxy for a political question: whether Marcos will direct enforcement or continue calibrated non-interference. The defences provide him procedural cover to do either. When the Supreme Court eventually rules — expected no earlier than June 2026 — that cover will narrow. Until then, and for every subsequent suspect, every argument that buys a day serves the same ultimate purpose. Understanding this does not change the legal analysis. The defences fail on the law regardless of why they are made. But it is essential to understanding what outcome their architects are actually seeking.

"The system that let him walk out at 2:30 in the morning — the Senate, the Supreme Court's writing period, the Interior Secretary's assurance, the gates that were open — was not a failure of institutions. It was institutions performing their primary function: protecting the people who control them."
